## Context

What is true that forces a decision. The situation, the constraint, the
pressure.

Write this so a reader in two years understands the problem without knowing the
project's history. State facts, not conclusions — if the context is written
well, the decision will feel inevitable, and if it does not, the decision may be
wrong.

## Decision

What we are doing, stated in one or two sentences that could be quoted in a code
review.

Then the specifics: what this means concretely, what it forbids, and how it is
enforced.

## Alternatives considered

The most valuable section. For each alternative: what it was, why it was
plausible, and why it lost.

A future engineer will have this idea. This section is how they discover it was
already considered.

### What this makes harder

State the costs honestly. An ADR that lists only benefits is advocacy, and the
next engineer will find the costs anyway — at a worse moment and without this
context.

### What this commits us to

Ongoing obligations. What must stay true for this decision to keep working.

## Enforcement

How this is verified. A mechanical check, a review obligation, or an honest
"convention only, not mechanically enforced."

Saying "not enforced" is fine and useful. Claiming enforcement that does not
exist is not.
